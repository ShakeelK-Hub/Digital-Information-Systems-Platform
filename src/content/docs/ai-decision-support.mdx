## Why this matters

Retail managers make decisions about stock, staffing, pricing and future demand. When sales, inventory and supplier information is reliable, information systems can help them identify patterns and compare options rather than relying only on guesswork.

Decision support systems and artificial intelligence can assist with these tasks, but they do not remove the need for human judgement. Results depend on the quality of the information, the suitability of the tool and the way its output is used.

:::tip[Key takeaway]
Use decision support to improve the evidence behind a decision. Treat AI output as something to evaluate, not something that must automatically be accepted.
:::

## Key concepts

### Decision support systems

A **decision support system (DSS)** helps people evaluate information and alternatives. It may use reports, dashboards, calculations, forecasts or scenarios. A DSS does not need to use AI; a well-designed dashboard can also support business decisions.

### Artificial intelligence

**Artificial intelligence (AI)** describes systems that can perform tasks such as identifying patterns, generating content, interpreting language or making predictions. Retail businesses might explore AI for demand forecasting, customer-service chatbots, trend analysis or identifying unusual transactions for human review.

AI is not automatically the best solution. A basic report or inventory alert may be cheaper, easier to maintain and sufficient for the problem.

### How decision support works

A typical process has three stages:

1. **Input:** collect relevant sales, stock, supplier and financial information.
2. **Analysis:** compare results, identify patterns or produce a forecast.
3. **Human decision:** review the output alongside business conditions and choose an action.

For example, a manager investigating repeated stock shortages can review recent sales, current inventory and supplier lead times before deciding how much to reorder.

## Levels of business decisions

| Level       | Typical focus                  | Retail example                                                 |
| ----------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Operational | Routine, day-to-day activities | Reordering a product that has reached its minimum stock level  |
| Tactical    | Short- to medium-term planning | Planning seasonal stock levels or staff schedules              |
| Strategic   | Longer-term direction          | Deciding whether to open another branch or expand online sales |

## Example from a retail business

A clothing shop in Pretoria frequently runs out of popular sizes while other sizes remain unsold. The owner first improves inventory records and uses a dashboard to compare sales by product, size and period.

Once enough reliable history is available, the shop could test a forecasting tool. The owner should also consider supplier lead times, available cash, seasonality and local demand before changing order quantities. The forecast informs the decision; it does not make the decision on the owner's behalf.

## Benefits and limitations

**Potential benefits**

- Better visibility of sales and stock patterns.
- More informed planning and replenishment.
- Less time spent on repetitive analysis.
- Earlier identification of unusual results or emerging trends.

**Potential limitations**

- Incomplete or inaccurate data can produce unreliable results.
- AI can generate errors or reflect bias in the data or design.
- Software, integration, training and maintenance add costs.
- Overreliance on automated recommendations can lead to poor decisions.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a reference for thinking about AI-related risks and their management. [[9]](/sources/#ref-9)

## Practical implementation steps

1. **Define the problem.** Identify a specific business issue, such as repeated stockouts or slow reporting.
2. **Check the data.** Improve accuracy, consistency and completeness before relying on analysis or predictions.
3. **Start with an appropriate tool.** A point-of-sale report, inventory alert or dashboard may be enough.
4. **Pilot AI where it adds value.** Test a limited use case before relying on it across the business.
5. **Compare output with actual results.** Monitor errors and adjust the process when results are unreliable.
6. **Train staff.** Explain the tool's purpose, limitations and appropriate use.
7. **Maintain human accountability.** Assign responsibility for reviewing recommendations and making consequential decisions.
8. **Review risks.** Consider cost, security, privacy, fairness and how the provider stores or uses submitted information.

Do not enter personal or confidential business information into an AI tool unless its use has been properly approved and assessed for lawful handling and security.

## How this connects to the other topics

AI and decision support depend on reliable data, making data quality a prerequisite rather than an afterthought. Privacy and ethical considerations also affect which information can be used and how it may be processed. Before adopting an AI provider, a business should assess its data practices, including retention, use of submitted information and any relevant cross-border transfers.
