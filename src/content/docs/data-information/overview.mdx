## Why this matters

A retail business generates data every day through sales, stock movements, supplier orders and customer transactions. However, collecting data is not enough. The information must be accurate, organised and relevant before the owner can use it to make sound decisions.

For example, if the stock system says a product is available when the shelf and storeroom are empty, the owner may promise a customer something the business cannot supply. Reliable information helps reduce these errors and supports better planning.

:::tip[Key takeaway]
Data becomes valuable when it is reliable, relevant and interpreted in the context of a business decision.
:::

## Key concepts

### Data, information and knowledge

- **Data** consists of recorded facts, such as individual sales, quantities and prices.
- **Information** is data organised to answer a question, such as total weekly sales by product.
- **Knowledge** develops when people interpret information alongside experience and business context.
- **Decisions and action** follow when that understanding is used to choose a response.

This relationship is often described through the data, information, knowledge and wisdom hierarchy. [[4]](/sources/#ref-4)

### The importance of data quality

Data quality concerns whether information is fit for its intended use. Important dimensions include accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness and validity. Poor data quality can weaken reports and lead to unreliable conclusions. [[1]](/sources/#ref-1) [[3]](/sources/#ref-3)

### The retail SME scenario

Consider a small clothing shop that uses a point-of-sale system, a spreadsheet for stock and a separate online storefront. If these records are not updated consistently, the owner may see conflicting sales and inventory figures.

Improving data quality and aligning records provides a stronger foundation for dashboards, integration and future automation.

## Example from the shop

The shop's inventory spreadsheet reports 12 units of a popular shirt. A physical count finds only 8. Before ordering more stock, the owner should investigate the four-unit difference by checking sales, returns, transfers and data-entry errors.

Correcting the records helps the owner make a more reliable replenishment decision.

## Practical steps

1. Identify the main business questions the data needs to answer.
2. List where relevant information is created and stored.
3. Check for missing, duplicate, outdated or conflicting records.
4. Assign responsibility for maintaining important records.
5. Review whether the information supports the decisions the owner needs to make.

## Quick self-check

- [ ] Can the business identify where its main records are stored?
- [ ] Are sales and inventory records checked for inconsistencies?
- [ ] Can the owner explain what a report measures and when its data was last updated?
- [ ] Are decisions based on evidence rather than unverified figures?
